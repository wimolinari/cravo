Glossary of terms

Complete glossary of technical and historical terms from the treatises

A compilation of terms with their equivalents in the original languages (Latin, Italian, Spanish, French) and definitions based on the translators' explanatory notes. Terms may appear throughout the treatise pages as annotated links — hover to see the definition. This page offers the complete glossary, browsable by category.

Instruments

Harpsichord clavecin · cembalo · clavicímbalo
Keyboard instrument in which strings are plucked by plectra (originally quills). Predominant from 1500 to 1800.
Clavichord monacordio · clavichord
Small metal tangents press the string. Allows dynamic nuance and even vibrato. Sancta Maria's preferred instrument.
Organ organo · orgue
Wind keyboard instrument producing sound through pipes. Couperin and Rameau confirm: their recommendations apply equally to the organ.
Spinet épinette · spinetta
Small harpsichord with oblique strings, ideal for beginners due to its light touch.
Pianoforte fortepiano
Forerunner of the modern piano, emerging around 1700.
Plectrum plume / emplumés
Small blade (originally a quill) that plucks the string. Couperin warns: beginners need soft plectra.
Viol viole — viola da gamba
Bowed string instrument with frets, played with a bow. Rameau mentions it for playing La Musette.

Technique and the body

Measure / Bar compás · battuta · mesure
In Sancta Maria: metre and pulse. In Couperin: "the quantity and equality of beats" — distinct from cadence.
Half-bar medio compás
The half of the bar (between the "downbeat" and the "upbeat").
Tablature tablature
For Couperin, the science of reading at sight. Also a system of notation using letters or numbers.
Legato liaison
Notes played without any gap between them. Couperin and Rameau cultivate it as the predominant articulation.
Aspiration aspiration (Couperin)
Intentional separation of a note — an expressive ornament. "More intense in light and rapid passages."
Suspension suspension (Couperin)
Intentional silence before a note, in gentle and slow pieces.
Cadence cadence · cadenza
In Couperin: "the spirit and the soul" combined with the beat — not the modern harmonic cadence.
Movement mouvement
In Couperin, synonymous with expressive cadence. Indicated by words: Tendrement, Vivement.
Good taste goût
Central principle of 18th-century French aesthetics. "The good taste of today is incomparably purer than the old." (Couperin)
Elegance buen ayre (Sancta Maria)
Seventh of the eight conditions. Includes rhythmic inequalities — a precursor of notes inégales.

Early ornaments (Sancta Maria)

Redouble (redobro) redoble
Ornament with alternating lower and upper notes (e–d–e–f–e). Applied to semibreves.
Reiterated quebro (Spanish ornament) quiebro reiterado
Like the redouble, but without the lower note (e–f–e–f–e). Equivalent to the simple trill. Applied to minims.
Simple quebro (Spanish ornament) quiebro simple
Short, of two or three notes — equivalent to the modern mordent.
Gloss glosa
Melodic variations or embellishments — written or improvised diminutions.
Diminution diminution
Substitution of a long note by a sequence of shorter notes. Core of the improvisatory practice of the 16th–17th centuries.

Modern ornaments (Couperin / Rameau)

Mordent pincé
Rapid alternation with the lower note, ending on the main note. Couperin: pincé-double.
Appoggiatura port-de-voix
Small note before the main note. Couperin distinguishes the old and modern manner; he recommends the modern one.
Trill tremblement · tremolo
Rapid alternation with the note above. In Couperin, it has three phases: the initial appoggiatura, the repercussions, and the point of rest.
Coulé coulé
In Couperin, three meanings: appoggiatura (notated), slide, or legato.
Repercussions batemens / battements
Alternating repeated notes that make up the "body" of an ornament.
Point of rest point d'arrêt
The final note of an ornament — where one "stops" on the main note. Marked by an asterisk.
Martèlement martèlement
Ornament of bowed string instruments. Couperin associates it with the double mordent of the harpsichord and organ.
Tenuto tenue
Sound sustained by one voice while others move (Rousseau).

Fingering and mechanics

Thumb crossing passage du pouce
The thumb passes under the other fingers for long scales. Already present in Sancta Maria (1565), theorized by Rameau.
Batterie batterie (Couperin/Rameau)
Playing the notes of a chord in succession — a broken chord.
Rolling passage roulement (Rameau)
A scale or rapid passage in stepwise motion.
Short octave octava encogida
A short octave — tuning of the lowest notes below their apparent pitches to extend the instrument's range.
Extended octave octava estendida
The regular keyboard octave, as opposed to the short octave.
Consonance consonancia
In Sancta Maria, a simultaneous interval (3rd, 5th, 6th, 8th) played with a single hand.

Early rhythmic values

Semibreve
"Whole note" — in Sancta Maria, roughly equivalent to the modern minim (half note). Redobros (redoubles) were performed on it.
Minim
In Sancta Maria, roughly equivalent to the modern crotchet (quarter note). Reiterated quebros were performed on it.
Crotchet (semiminim)
In Sancta Maria, roughly equivalent to the modern quaver (eighth note).
Quaver
In Sancta Maria, roughly equivalent to the modern semiquaver (sixteenth note).
Fusa
Thirty-second note. Frescobaldi mentions it in the context of "ends of passages."

Style and forms

Toccata toccata
Free Italian form. Frescobaldi: "this manner of playing must not be subject to the beat."
Partita
In Frescobaldi: variations on a melody or bass. They call for a slower tempo in passages with expressive affect.
Passacaglia passacaglia
Variations over a descending ostinato bass. Frescobaldi: "they may be played separately, in whatever manner pleases most."
Chaconne ciaccona
Variations over a short bass, generally in triple metre. Frescobaldi was possibly the first to explore the Passacaglia–Chaconne as a pair.
Prelude prélude
For Couperin: "a free composition in which the imagination gives itself free rein." He composed eight.
Mesuré mesuré
Couperin's indication: a prelude played with metric precision, without rhythmic freedom.
Allemande allemande
German dance in moderate common time. The first piece of French suites.
Courante courante · corrente
Dance in triple metre; the French version is slow with hemiolas; the Italian version is fast.
Gigue gigue · giga
Fast dance in compound metre. Concludes Baroque suites.
Rondeau rondeau
Form with a refrain alternating with contrasting episodes (couplets).
Episode couplet
Each of the contrasting sections of a rondeau.

Compositional styles

Stylus fantasticus stylus phantasticus
Free style idiomatic to keyboard music. Frescobaldi is its greatest representative in Italy.
Ricercare
Strict contrapuntal form, originating in the vocal music of Palestrina and transferred to the keyboard.
Canzona
Imitative instrumental form, derived from the French chanson.
Fantasy
Free composition or improvisation in imitative style. "Tañer fantasía" is Sancta Maria's goal.
Tiento tiento
Iberian keyboard form, related to the ricercare and the fantasy.
Singing affects affetti cantabili
Key concept of Frescobaldi — playing in imitation of the expressiveness of singing.
Durezza durezze
In Frescobaldi: suspensions or dissonances. They must be rearticulated to sustain the sound.

Historical systems

Hexachord hexachord
Ascending sequence of six notes (T–T–S–T–T). Syllables ut–re–mi–fa–sol–la. Formalized by Guido d'Arezzo.
Mutation
The shift from one hexachord to another when the melody exceeds the interval of a sixth.
Solmization
System of naming notes with syllables (ut–re–mi–fa–sol–la) rather than letters.
Church modes
The eight (later twelve) modes of Gregorian chant, in parallel with the emerging tonal system.
Musica ficta
Alteration of notes (sharps, flats) not written but implied by convention.

Historical figures

Antonio de Cabezón (1510–1566)
Spanish royal organist; consulted by Sancta Maria. His work would have received Cabezón's approval.
Johann Jacob Froberger (1616–1667)
German pupil of Frescobaldi (from 1637). He forged a personal idiom drawing on the Italian, French, and German traditions.
Jan Pieterszoon Sweelinck (1562–1621)
Dutch composer and organist — together with Frescobaldi, the most influential keyboard composer of the first half of the 17th century.
Athanasius Kircher (1602–1680)
Jesuit theorist. He divided keyboard music into three styles: ancient, dance, and fantastical.
Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778)
Philosopher. His Dictionnaire de Musique (1768) is a recurring source in the translators' notes.
Nikolaus Harnoncourt (1929–2016)
Austrian conductor, pioneer of historically informed performance. "He sought not an archaeologically correct performance, but a living and worthy one."
