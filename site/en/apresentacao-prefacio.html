The volumes are published in print and digital formats, with national and international distribution. The print version is kindly distributed to libraries, universities, and other institutions of an educational, scientific, and cultural nature, both in Brazil and abroad. The Editorial Committee is composed of the members of the Program's Deliberative Commission. Each publication assembles its Scientific Commission in accordance with participation in the realization of the respective projects, also drawing on the collaboration of colleagues from Brazil and abroad. The Editorial Committee and the Scientific Commission are engaged in this institutional project, rooted in an academic tradition that makes excellence its foremost priority. We are grateful for the dedication of the entire team that made this undertaking possible, and we hope that this editorial line will afford the reader a meaningful encounter with musical research.

Preface

Marcelo Fagerlande

Coordinator of the UFRJ Harpsichord Week · Editor of this volume

In 2003 we established the Harpsichord specialization within the Graduate Program of the School of Music of the Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro, as part of the Master's Degree in Music. The following year we conceived the First Harpsichord Week, an event that has since taken place annually without interruption. In 2013 we celebrated the tenth anniversary of that gathering, whose principal aim is to bring together students and faculty from teaching institutions throughout the country that offer instruction on the instrument, whether at the federal, state, or municipal level. Participating students are at various stages of study, ranging from professional diploma programs through undergraduate and master's degrees up to the doctoral level.

One of the hallmarks of the Harpsichord Week has been its promotion of academic debate on diverse aspects of the harpsichord through the most recent research involving the instrument. The principal focus lies in the investigation and recovery of its history, especially in Brazil, as well as in questions of pedagogy and performance practice. Alongside these debates — conducted through round tables with invited faculty — the Harpsichord Week has also offered recitals by students from the participating institutions, such as the Universidade Estadual de Campinas, the Universidade Federal de Pernambuco, the Escola de Música de Brasília, the Escola Municipal de Música de São Paulo, the Conservatório de Tatuí (SP), and the School of Music of the Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro, host of the event.

One of the seminars offered to the first cohort of the Master's in Harpsichord at UFRJ, in 2004, was entitled "Selected Keyboard Treatises," and on that occasion the works of Thomas de Sancta Maria, Girolamo Frescobaldi, François Couperin, Jean-Philippe Rameau, and Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach were studied. The translation of these authors' texts arose naturally, as the consequence of the dedication of a group eager to uncover some of the most important recommendations on keyboard technique and interpretation from the sixteenth, seventeenth, and eighteenth centuries. That group included the names who today, alongside us, form the team responsible for the final version published here: Ana Cecilia Tavares, Clara Albuquerque, Maria Aida Barroso, and Mayra Pereira.

We believe there could be no better way to celebrate the ten years of the Harpsichord Week than with the publication of these translations. The works studied constitute some of the most important references for our instrument, and making them available in Portuguese may assist the many harpsichord students and scholars who have grown in number in recent years in Brazil, thanks both to the emergence of new programs and to the consolidation of existing ones. It is likely, however, that the interested readership will prove still broader, since these invaluable technical and interpretive recommendations may also prove useful in the study of other keyboard instruments, such as the organ, the clavichord, the fortepiano, and even the modern piano.

On the treatises — a historical overview

The production of music treatises occurred primarily during the Baroque period, as Bukofzer already observed, which can be seen in the quantity of works devoted to the teaching of instruments, counterpoint, thoroughbass, and also those that approach music from a standpoint of theoretical speculation. Such treatises, in general, may be grouped according to the three disciplines of music — musica theorica, musica poetica, and musica practica — which revive an ancient Aristotelian classification to which authors still adhered. The first category encompasses treatises that study the nature of sound and music, aesthetic inquiries into the situation and function of music at the center of the totality of human knowledge, and metaphysical speculations on the harmony of the universe. The second category concerns treatises containing practical rules of counterpoint, thoroughbass, and composition. The third group comprises treatises intended for teaching and performance. It is within this last group that the works translated here may be placed.

Just as it can be difficult to classify whether a given work is a "theoretical" or "practical" treatise, the same may be said of the very term "treatise." In its strict sense, the word denotes "a developed work that systematically expounds the principles of a science or art." A "method," by contrast, designates "an elementary treatise." In our selection we find both more extensive works and others that, despite their value, are more concise and present only the essence of the proposed subject. For this reason, although all may be encompassed by the broader term, we have chosen to describe our collection as "treatises" and "methods."

The large number of Baroque treatises does not necessarily imply uniformity of quality: it is common for many to merely repeat what others had already written before them. The collection assembled here, however, is special insofar as Sancta Maria's work is the oldest and most detailed description of keyboard technique, while the others — though varying greatly in length — contain reflections by the most notable keyboard composers of their respective times and places: Frescobaldi in seventeenth-century Italy; François Couperin and Rameau in eighteenth-century France.

The focus of interest in treatises, broadly speaking, shifts over time. While at the beginning of the seventeenth century the emphasis of works directed at instruments or voice lay in how to ornament or realize diminutions, from the middle of that century there began to appear treatises and methods devoted no longer to several instruments indiscriminately, but to one in particular. Sancta Maria's treatise, for example, published at the end of the sixteenth century, illustrates the focus on the technique of diminutions and improvisation. The works of Couperin and Rameau, dedicated specifically to the harpsichord, are clearly concerned with questions of performance at the instrument.

I

Book Called the Art of Playing Fantasia

Thomas de Sancta Maria (Madrid, ? — Ribadavia, 1570), Spanish theorist and composer, became a friar in the Dominican Order of Santa Maria de Atocha in Madrid in 1536. He served as organist in various monasteries of Castile, principally in Valladolid, where he probably encountered Antonio and Juan de Cabezón, royal organists, with whom he conversed and exchanged ideas while preparing his Libro Llamado Arte de tañer Fantasía. According to the treatise's own frontispiece, the work had been examined and approved by the two eminent musicians.

Sancta Maria apparently began writing his work in 1541, and had it licensed in 1557, but due to a shortage of paper he was only able to publish it in 1565, in Valladolid. In the first part the author presents the rudiments of music up to Chapter XII. Chapters XIII through XIX, which deal with keyboard technique, address hand position, touch, articulation, fingering, two types of ornament, and the use of dotted notes. In Chapters XX through XXIII, Sancta Maria discusses the performance of composed works and the application of diminutions.

Among the qualities of the treatise most frequently mentioned are its originality and systematic organization. The Libro Llamado Arte de tañer Fantasía is considered to be directed primarily at the clavichord, specifically mentioned by the author, who nevertheless does not exclude the possibility of other keyboard instruments in his text. Thus, his technical recommendations are applicable and useful for studying the repertoire of this period on the harpsichord or organ as well.

II

To the Reader

Girolamo Frescobaldi (Ferrara, 1583 — Rome, 1643), Italian composer, harpsichordist, and organist, is considered, together with Sweelinck, the most influential keyboard composer of the first half of the seventeenth century. Contemporaries remarked on his discovery of a new style of playing, especially on the harpsichord, and his fame extended beyond the borders of Italy. In Germany he would become the most influential and most copied keyboard composer during the second half of the seventeenth century: in 1637 Froberger moved to Rome in order to become his pupil; his contrapuntal works were copied by none other than the young J. S. Bach and later by his followers.

Although he also wrote a substantial vocal output, Frescobaldi was extraordinarily famous in his time as a keyboard virtuoso, combining the qualities of both performer and composer. The new elements that contemporaries identified in his style were probably associated with his own manner of playing the harpsichord, particularly the toccatas. His preface, with its wealth of information about the new mode of playing, shows how keenly aware he was of the need to explain the interpretation of a newly created style.

Frescobaldi's vast keyboard output, published in several volumes from 1608 to 1645, contains canzonas, ricercares, dances, and variations, in addition to toccatas. His preface, To the Reader, was published multiple times in various versions in the two books of toccatas. Having undergone revisions over time, we present here its final version, published in Book II of toccatas in 1637.

III

The Art of Playing the Harpsichord

François Couperin (Paris, 1668 — Paris, 1733), composer, harpsichordist, and organist, was the most important member of the Couperin family — one of the great musical dynasties, active in Paris and its environs from the late sixteenth century to the mid-nineteenth century. The Couperins were particularly associated with the Church of St. Gervais, where they held the post of organist for 173 years. François, also called "the Great", was the author of some of the finest music of the French classical school, and may be cited as the most important musical figure in France between Lully and Rameau.

The printing of his harpsichord works extends from 1707 to 1730. In 1717 he published the definitive version of L'Art de toucher le Clavecin — the source used for our translation — a work considered indispensable for those who dedicate themselves to the instrument. The method is divided into five parts: posture of the body and hands; ornaments useful in performance; short exercises for the right and left hand; observations on how to choose good fingering for difficult passages; eight diversified Preludes.

A characteristic of the text that immediately strikes the reader when consulting L'Art de toucher le Clavecin in the original — and that naturally poses challenges for translation into any other language — is Couperin's style, which makes no literary pretensions and presents grammatical difficulties in his use of the French language. The illustrious composer was not alone in this, for the same was true of painters, architects, physicians, lawyers, and scientists of his time. The author himself offered a self-critique: "Je demande grace aux Sçavans, et aux Puristes sur le stile de mes Préfaces: je n'y parle que de mon Art..." ("I ask indulgence of the learned and the purists concerning the style of my prefaces: in them I speak only of my Art...").

It is a work that conveys unequivocally Couperin's love of the harpsichord and his firm convictions regarding the instrument's qualities: precision, clarity, definition, brilliance, and range. It also demonstrates his desire to render it as expressive as any other musical instrument.

IV

On the Mechanics of the Fingers on the Harpsichord

Jean-Philippe Rameau (Dijon, 1683 — Paris, 1764), French composer, theorist, organist, and harpsichordist, was the leading figure of French music in his time, especially in dramatic genres, and an important innovator in harmonic theory.

In his second publication of harpsichord pieces, the title of the first printing, issued in Paris in 1724, already highlights the mechanics of the fingers: Pièces de clavessin avec une méthode pour la méchanique des doigts ou l'on enseigne les moyens de se procurer une parfaite execution sur cet intrument ("Harpsichord Pieces with a Method for the Mechanics of the Fingers, in which one is taught the means of achieving a perfect execution on this instrument"). Indeed, the first subject appearing immediately after this frontispiece is the text on the mechanics of the fingers.

In De la Mécanique des doigts sur le Clavessin we find mention of various parts of the human body related to playing — fingers, hands, arms, elbows. The author's primary objective is the pursuit of a precise and secure technique, always seeking the maximum degree of relaxation. With regard to touch, Rameau associates attentiveness to the weight of the hands and its effect on the fingers with the means of obtaining a beautiful tone. Like Couperin, Rameau cultivates legato as the predominant articulation.

Rameau's innovative character as a composer is also evident in his indications of harpsichord technique: in contrast to his contemporaries, he prescribes only modern fingerings. Similarly, he advocates the use of thumb crossing, as Bach did in Germany. One of his most essential ideas is his assertion that "a large movement should only take place if a smaller one is not sufficient."

Gilbert concludes that "Rameau offers in a few pages the clearest and most detailed description of keyboard technique of the eighteenth century which, when read together with Couperin's L'Art de toucher le Clavecin, forms the foundation of the practice of the entire period."

Concluding remarks

We naturally recognize the importance and usefulness of sources such as treatises for the study of music from earlier centuries — justified by the simple fact that within Western culture there is no continuous, unbroken musical tradition. We do, however, identify certain precautions that should be kept in mind:

  • Many authors of the period acknowledge the limitations of written works, invoking the advantages of direct contact between master and pupil.
  • Frequently the comprehension of treatise content may be hindered, since many terms have lost their original meanings.
  • Certain self-evident matters of the era, understandably, were neither explained nor even mentioned in the treatises. But, as Staier reminds us, precisely because they are no longer familiar to us, they are the ones that lead us to important reflections.
We suggest to the performer, when recreating the repertoire of earlier centuries, that alongside the study of treatises, they not undervalue their own intuition in constructing their interpretation, especially when they already possess considerable experience. This balance may lead to the result sought by Harnoncourt, who always pursued not an archaeologically correct performance, but one that is alive and worthy. — Marcelo Fagerlande

Translation criteria

For our translation of the works of Sancta Maria, Frescobaldi, Couperin, and Rameau, we used the editions of 1565, 1637, 1717, and 1724, respectively, consulted in facsimile publications. We sought to preserve the original style of each author, even when the phrasing might depart from the way the idea would be expressed in contemporary Portuguese.

Our approach diverges from certain translations of these works into other languages, in which we perceived a preference for a text more explanatory of its content. By choosing this other path, we believe we are offering present-day readers the possibility of a closer engagement with the works and with the thinking of their authors.

  • In some cases a change in punctuation was necessary, in order to achieve better conformity with Portuguese grammatical norms.
  • Words added to the original text have been indicated by square brackets.
  • We have chosen to translate the majority of musical terms, frequently including notes with the terms in their original languages.
  • Musical examples have been edited using the clefs most commonly employed today, and corrections have been indicated by square brackets.
  • At the end of Couperin's work there are critical notes on the examples, the Allemande, and the Preludes.
  • We have included as an appendix the ornament tables of Couperin and Rameau, presented at the end of their respective works.
