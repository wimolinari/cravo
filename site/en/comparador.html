Side-by-Side Comparator

Four authors discussing the same concept, in parallel

Compare directly how each author approaches the same elements of technique — the fastest way to make the "synapse" between the Spanish, Italian, and French traditions.

Posture at the instrument

Sancta Maria 1565

Hands "curved like a cat's claw (mão de gato)"; arched fingers. Elbows close to the body, without tension. Thumb dropped and folded inward.

Frescobaldi 1637

Does not address posture — the focus is on style and tempo in the performance of toccatas.

Couperin 1717

Forearm, wrist, and fingers level. An adult seated 22.5 cm from the waist. Centre of the body aligned with the centre of the keyboard. Right foot well turned out. Do not beat time with the head.

Rameau 1724

Progressive elbow position: above the keyboard at the beginning (the hand "falls"); below when the technique matures (the hand "clings" to the keys for legato).

How to attack the key

Sancta Maria 1565

Fingertip flesh (not nails), at the edges; firm, with impetus, but without striking from above. Fingers kept close to the keys.

Frescobaldi 1637

Does not detail the attack. What matters is the rhetorical articulation between passages.

Couperin 1717

"The sweetness of touch depends also on keeping the fingers as close as possible to the keys." Soft plectra recommended for beginners.

Rameau 1724

"Let the fingers fall upon the keys rather than striking them. Let them flow from one to the next, in succession." The hand supports the fingers, never the reverse.

Fingering for scales

Sancta Maria 1565

Alternating 3-4 (ascending in the right hand: 3434...), already with thumb-under passing in longer eighth notes.

Frescobaldi 1637

Does not document fingerings. 17th-century Italian practice favored "3 over 2."

Couperin 1717

Retains the old "3 over 2" system, but introduces finger substitution on a single note for refined legato.

Rameau 1724

Modern fingerings only. Thumb-under passing is central. Avoid playing sharps or flats with fingers 1 or 5.

The trill — where to begin?

Sancta Maria 1565

"Quebro reiterado de mínimas" (repeated quebro on half notes). "For great refinement, it is now customary to begin from the note above" — the earliest known documentation of the modern trill.

Frescobaldi 1637

"Let the trill be fast and the [contralateral] passage expressive and less quickly." Rhythmic independence between the hands.

Couperin 1717

Three phases: (1) appui (leaning on the upper note), (2) repercussions, (3) point d'arrêt (stopping point). Begin more slowly and accelerate imperceptibly.

Rameau 1724

Begin by raising the fingers as high as possible; gradually reduce until reaching a "lively and light movement." Do not rush the cadence: it closes by itself with practice.

Tempo, rhythm, and expression

Sancta Maria 1565

Three ways of playing eighth notes: dotting the 1st (standard); rushing the 1st (Lombard — "much more graceful"); rushing 3 and dotting the 4th ("the most graceful of all").

Frescobaldi 1637

"This manner of playing should not be subject to the beat." The pulse bends to the affect, as in madrigals. Cadences are sustained. Pause before double-hand passages.

Couperin 1717

A founding distinction: mesure (the meter) ≠ cadence (the soul). Notes inégales: "we dot eighth notes moving by step, yet we notate them as equal."

Rameau 1724

Strict equality in practice. "Lightness and speed are only achieved through this equality of motion. By rushing, one flees from the very thing one seeks."

The expressive ideal

Sancta Maria 1565

"So that all music may have that grace and life which it deserves, it is necessary to play with the utmost refinement."

Frescobaldi 1637

"To play with cantabile affects and with a variety of passages" — imitating the expressiveness of singing.

Couperin 1717

"To give soul to the instrument" through the cessation and suspension of sounds — made at precisely the right moment.

Rameau 1724

"It is not enough to feel the effects of an art; one must understand them in a way that renders them intelligible."

Fingering for ornaments (Redouble / Trill)

Sancta Maria 1565

Redouble (redobro) with 3 fingers. Right hand: 2 3 4 3, begins and ends on 3. Left hand variants: 3-2-1 or 4-3-2.

Frescobaldi 1637

Does not prescribe fingering.

Couperin 1717

Most common trills: 3-2 and 4-3 in the right hand; 1-2 and 2-3 in the left. Advises young students to "practice trills with all fingers."

Rameau 1724

Does not prescribe specific finger pairs — focuses on movement: raise the fingers very high at the start; reduce gradually.

Age and study

Sancta Maria 1565

Does not address age. The treatise is directed at "new teachers" learning to improvise.

Frescobaldi 1637

Does not address age. Presupposes an already trained performer.

Couperin 1717

"6 to 7 years" is the ideal age — "for shaping the hands in harpsichord technique, the earlier the better." A footstool is recommended for young children.

Rameau 1724

"Assiduous and well-guided practice [...] and a little time will inevitably compensate for less naturally gifted fingers." Everyone can attain perfection.

A defining phrase from each author

Sancta Maria 1565

"Striking the keys well is also one of the most essential and principal things in playing."

Frescobaldi 1637

"This manner of playing should not be subject to the beat, [...] according to the affects or meaning of the words."

Couperin 1717

"Good execution depends far more on flexibility and great freedom of the fingers than on strength."

Rameau 1724

"The greatest movement should only occur to the extent that a lesser one is not sufficient."
