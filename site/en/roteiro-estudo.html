Harpsichordist's Study Guide

Six stages combining the four treatises

The four treatises were not written as a single curriculum, but it is possible to combine them into a pedagogical sequence that draws the best from each author. This path was designed for a student starting from scratch or who already plays another keyboard instrument and wishes to master baroque harpsichord technique.

★ How to use this study guide

Durations are indicative — adaptable to the time available and the student's age. A motivated adult student can work through all stages in approximately 18 months. Children may take 3–4 years. Each stage includes an immediate practical focus and specific references for returning to the treatises.

Stage 1 2–4 weeks

Posture, hands, and the First Lesson

Before any piece, establish the physical foundation. Rameau is the guide here — his First Lesson is the most solid technical foundation in the literature.

First Lesson position (right hand)

  • Elbows above keyboard level, according to Rameau (1st stage).
  • Fingers 1 and 5 at the edges of the keys. The other fingers curve naturally.
  • Flexible wrist; "dead" hand supporting the fingers.
  • Each finger depresses a key without moving the others. Lifting and attacking are simultaneous.
Daily practice (15–20 min) Five fingers over five keys. Each hand separately; then together. Variations: now 4 notes, now 6, now 8. Contrary motion between the hands. Maintain absolute evenness of movement — do not rush.

Stage 2 4–6 weeks

Scales and thumb crossing

Once the First Lesson is secure in both hands, expand to long scales. Here Sancta Maria and Rameau work together.

Right hand ascending (Rameau) — thumb crossing between F and G

  • Scales with alternating 3–4 (Sancta Maria, Ch. XVIII): the "general, without exception" rule for the ascending right hand.
  • Thumb crossing in long scales — Rameau requires it; Sancta Maria already documents it.
  • Avoid playing sharps/flats with fingers 1 or 5 (Rameau).
Daily practice C major scales in two octaves, hands separately. Then G and F. In all: rhythmic evenness above all else. When fluent, join the hands.
↗ Sancta Maria · Ch. XVIII ↗ Topic · Fingering
Stage 3 2–3 months

First pieces and basic ornaments

Time for the first real pieces. Couperin suggests beginning with the Allemande from Part 3 of the treatise — conceived precisely as a transitional piece. Begin also the study of the most common ornaments.

  • Ornament table from Couperin's First Book (appendix of the treatise).
  • Mordent (pincé) and trill (tremblement) — both beginning on the note above.
  • Trills: 3–2 and 4–3 in the right hand; 1–2 and 2–3 in the left hand (Couperin).
  • Practice trills slowly at first: lift the fingers very high initially, gradually reducing the movement (Rameau).
Recommended entry pieces
Daily practice 45 min: 15 min First Lesson + scales; 15 min isolated ornaments; 15 min on the piece you are studying — bar by bar, slowly.
↗ Couperin · Ornaments ↗ Topic · Ornaments
Stage 4 3–4 months

Rhythm, notes inégales, and expression

Once the basic technique is secure, begin working with what is not written: rhythmic inequalities, rhetorical articulation, and Couperin's aspiration.

  • Notes inégales applied subtly to stepwise eighth notes (Couperin justifies them; Sancta Maria had already described them).
  • Sancta Maria · Ch. XIX: three manners of eighth notes. Begin with the first (dotting the 1st, rushing the 2nd).
  • Aspiration and suspension in Couperin: practice releasing the preceding note slightly before beginning the next phrase.
  • Character of the piece guides the rhythm: Tendrement, Vivement, Mesuré.
Daily practice Play the same phrase in 3 ways: strictly even, with a slight inégalité, with a more marked inégalité. Listen to the difference. Use your phone's recorder to hear yourself from the outside.
↗ Sancta Maria · Elegance ↗ Couperin · Expression ↗ Topic · Rhythm and tempo
Stage 5 3–4 months

Frescobaldi and the stylus fantasticus

Time to immerse yourself in 17th-century Italy and understand free time from the inside. Frescobaldi's nine instructions are not technical directives — they are principles of performance that reorganize everything you have learned.

  • Read Frescobaldi's preface in full until you know it by heart. Return to it always.
  • Study a short Toccata (Toccata I from Book I, for example) with the score in hand.
  • Practice arpeggiation at opening gestures and re-attacking dissonances.
  • Sustained cadences: expressive ritardando at the end of each section.
  • Trill in one hand, passage in the other: keep them independent.
Study with benchmark recordings
Weekly practice Play an entire toccata and stop whenever you wish — Frescobaldi permits it. Identify each "passi" (passage) and its cadences. Compare your version with those of 3 different harpsichordists.
↗ Frescobaldi · To the Reader
Stage 6 a lifetime

Repertoire, autonomy, and good taste

The treatises end here — your study does not. From this stage onward, the treatises become a permanent reference, consulted as the repertoire demands.

  • Build a repertoire of 3–5 pieces by each composer that you know by heart and can play on any instrument.
  • Work on Rameau's crossed battery figures (Les Tourbillons, Les Cyclopes) — the technical heights of the French style.
  • Learn figured bass / accompaniment only now — Couperin recommends waiting 2–3 years before this stage.
  • Study the works of Froberger (a pupil of Frescobaldi) — a synthesis of Italian, French, and German traditions.
  • Return to Couperin's treatise every 6 months — its depth is only revealed through successive readings.
"Que se lembre bem que quanto mais se persevera nos primeiros princípios, mais se avança na carreira, pois aquele a quem esses princípios desagrada é quase sempre vítima de sua impaciência." — Rameau
Permanent habit Whenever you feel your technique is getting rusty: return to the First Lesson. Whenever you feel your expression is becoming rigid: re-read Frescobaldi's nine instructions. Whenever you feel your taste is growing thin: study a chapter of Couperin. Whenever you sit at a new keyboard: play a Prelude.
