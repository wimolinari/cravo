III

L'Art de toucher le Clavecin

François Couperin · Paris, 1717 · L'Art de toucher le Clavecin

François Couperin (Paris, 1668 — 1733), "le Grand", is the most important musical figure in France between Lully and Rameau. His method, published between the First and Second Books of Pieces, is "essential reading" for anyone devoted to the harpsichord.

📖 This edition

The present translation is based on the definitive edition of 1717 — which, compared to the first (1716), includes a new frontispiece, six supplementary pages, and textual modifications. Couperin combines a pedagogical treatise with intimate reflections on the life of a harpsichord master.

Recommended listening — complete works by Christophe Rousset

Preface

Plan of this method

"The Method I present here is unique and bears no relation to Tablature, which is merely a science of numbers. Here I deal with every aspect of good touch at the Harpsichord. [...] There is yet an infinite distance between Tablature and the manner of playing well." — Couperin, preface

Couperin warns: this is not a technical manual — it is a book about how to play well. He announces five parts:

  1. The position of the body and hands.
  2. The ornaments that serve performance.
  3. Short, essential preliminary exercises.
  4. Observations on fingering, with reference to the First and Second Books.
  5. Eight diversified Preludes, in order of difficulty, with numbered fingers.
Part 1

Position of the body and hands

Age to begin

"The appropriate age to begin teaching children is six to seven years: not that this should exclude older persons, but, naturally, to form the hands at the harpsichord, the earlier the better."

Posture at the instrument

  • Forearm, wrist, and fingers level; the chair should allow this.
  • Under children's feet, a support so they are not left dangling.
  • Distance from the keyboard for an adult: approximately 22.5 cm from the waist.
  • The centre of the body aligned with the centre of the keyboard.
  • Turn as little as possible to the right; feet side by side, with the right foot particularly turned outward.
  • Do not fix the gaze; "look at the people present around you as though you were not occupied with anything else".
  • Do not mark the beat with the head, body, or feet.

💡 Couperin's tip for children

"For my part, when teaching children, I take the precaution of carrying the key of the instrument on which I instruct them, so that in my absence they cannot undo in a single moment what I have carefully taught during three quarters of an hour."

The right instrument

"One should initially use a Spinet or a single-manual Harpsichord in early childhood, and both should have their plectra regulated very lightly. [...] Good playing depends far more on the flexibility and great freedom of the fingers than on strength."

The coupled harpsichord (with both manuals linked) requires more force — dangerous for a child, who "will strain their little hands to make the keys speak", leading to "badly placed hands and heaviness of touch".

Part 1 (cont.)

Pedagogical reflections

On trills

"It is necessary to use preferably the less capable [fingers], without regard to the old manner of fingering, which must be abandoned in favour of today's manner of playing well."

On tablature and memory

"One should not begin to teach Tablature to children until they have a certain number of Pieces in their hands. [...] Memory develops far better when one learns by heart."

On flexibility

"The hands of women are generally better. [...] The flexibility of the nerves contributes far more to playing well than strength."

Part 4

How to choose fingering

Couperin establishes the numbering he himself described as differing from his usual custom:

Finger numbering

Left hand: 5 4 3 2 1   |   Right hand: 1 2 3 4 5

Thumb = 1, little finger = 5. A convention that persists to this day.

"One will learn through practice how useful it is to change one finger for another on the same note, and what legato effect this produces in playing."

Finger substitution on the same note is, for Couperin, one of the keys to modern legato. He uses it systematically to connect chained trills.

Central reflection

Aspiration and suspension — giving soul to the harpsichord

"Being the sounds of the Harpsichord each individually fixed, and consequently incapable of being increased or diminished, it had seemed almost impossible until now to give soul to this instrument."

The founding question: how to be expressive on an instrument that has no dynamics?

Couperin's answer is original and modernist:

Aspiration aspiration

The separation of a note — an expressive micro-pause. More intense in light and fast passages, more subtle in gentle and slow passages.

Suspension suspension

A silence before a note — used almost exclusively in gentle and slow pieces. "The silence that precedes the note must be guided by the taste of the performer."

"These two ornaments, by their contrast, leave the ear uncertain, so that on occasions when bowed instruments increase their sounds, the suspension of the Harpsichord's sounds seems — by a contrary effect — to bring to the ear the desired impression."

Part 2

Ornaments that serve performance

"It is the value of the notes that must, in general, determine the duration of double mordents, double appoggiaturas, and trills."

Mordent pincé

"Every mordent must end on the note over which it is placed." The beats and the final note are all contained within the value of the main note. On the harpsichord and organ, it serves the function of the martèlement of bowed instruments.

Appoggiatura port-de-voix

Composed of two notes of value and one small ornamental note. Couperin advocates the modern manner (with finger substitution) over the old, as it produces more legato.

Trill tremblement

Consists of three elements: (1) the appoggiatura on the note above the principal; (2) the beats; (3) the point of repose. It must "begin more slowly than it ends, but this gradation must be imperceptible".

★ Rule of the trill

"No matter on what note a trill is marked; one must always begin it on the tone or semitone above." — This rule was standard until around 1800.

Linked thirds: old manner vs. modern manner

★ Old vs. modern

Old manner of several consecutive thirds: "produces no legato whatsoever".

Modern manner: connects the thirds by means of finger substitution on the same note.

"I am persuaded that few people in Paris remain intractable regarding old principles, Paris being the centre of what is good."

The Allemande — transitional piece to the first idiomatic works

In Part 3, Couperin introduces an Allemande "légèrement" that he composed especially for the method — conceived as a bridge between the exercises and the actual pieces of Books I and II.

The experiment of two simultaneous trills

"One day, while having a young person practise them, I tried letting her play both trills at the same time with the same hand. Her natural aptitude, excellent hands, and great acquired skill brought her to the point of playing them very regularly."

★ Principle for choosing fingering

"As the 2nd and 4th of these four slurred notes are the ones that suggest the true harmony in relation to the bass, it is necessary that they be played with the same fingers, as though the melody were simple and without intervening notes."

The fingering reveals the underlying harmonic structure. Notes belonging to the harmony receive the same fingers as the main notes, marking them audibly.

Part 5

The eight Preludes

"A Prelude is a free composition in which the imagination abandons itself to everything that presents itself. But, as it is very rare to find geniuses capable of improvising, those who have recourse to these written Preludes must play them in a natural manner, without adhering too strictly to exact time; unless I have indicated it explicitly by the word Mesuré."

Couperin composed eight Preludes in the keys of the pieces from his Books I and II. The first four are short (one page); the remaining four are twice the length.

"One might venture to say that, among many subjects, Music, in comparison to Poetry, has its prose and its verse."

Remarks between the Preludes

"Although these Preludes have been written in measured time, there is nonetheless a manner of using them that must be followed. I explain: a Prelude is a free composition in which the imagination abandons itself to everything that presents itself. But, as it is very rare to find geniuses capable of improvising, those who have recourse to these written Preludes must play them in a natural manner, without adhering too strictly to exact time; unless I have indicated it explicitly by the word Mesuré." — Couperin, remarks between the Preludes

"One of the reasons these Preludes were written in measured time is the ease one will find, whether in teaching or in learning them."

Conclusion on how to play the Harpsichord

Couperin concludes the section on the Preludes with a synthesis of his entire aesthetic for the instrument:

"To conclude regarding how to play the Harpsichord in general, my feeling is that one must not depart from the character that suits this instrument: passages, batteries that lie comfortably under the hands; lute-like and syncopated parts, which should be preferred to those that are full of slurs or excessively low notes. One must maintain a perfect legato in everything one plays; all ornaments must be quite precise, and those composed of beats must be executed very regularly, through an imperceptible gradation. Care must be taken not to alter the tempo in Pieces where it is set, and not to dwell on notes whose duration has ended. Finally, let one's playing be fashioned according to the good taste of today, which is incomparably purer than the old." — Couperin, general conclusion on the harpsichord

★ Synthesis of Couperin's aesthetic

  • Prose vs. verse — Preludes = prose (free); Pieces = verse (measured).
  • Character proper to the harpsichord — passages, batteries, lute-like and syncopated parts.
  • Perfect legato.
  • Precise ornaments, with imperceptible gradation.
  • Do not alter the tempo in pieces where it is set.
  • Do not dwell on notes whose duration has already ended.
  • The good taste of today — incomparably purer than the old.

Final reflection

The harpsichord, the Sonatas, and the French good taste

Couperin meditated at length on the harpsichord's relationship to the emerging Italian style:

"This instrument has its particularities, just as the Violin has its own. [...] If the Harpsichord cannot swell its sound, [...] it has in turn other advantages which are precision, clarity, brilliance, and compass."

Metre and cadence

★ Fundamental distinction

Metre = "the quantity and equality of the beats".

Cadence = "precisely, the spirit and the soul that must be combined with it".

"We confuse metre with what is called cadence or movement."

It is this "soul" — irreducible to notation — that leads the French to indicate Tendrement, Vivement at the beginning of pieces. Couperin laments:

"There are deficiencies in our way of writing music that correspond to the way of writing our language. The fact is that we write differently from how we play. This is what causes foreigners to play our music less well than we play theirs."

The famous practical remark on equally-notated quavers:

"For example, we dot several consecutive quavers moving by step, yet we notate them as equal. We submit to this practice and so it continues."

This is the theoretical basis of the French notes inégales — quavers moving by step are notated as equal but performed as dotted (unequal).

On accompaniment

"One should wait two or three years before learning accompaniment. [...] The vivacity with which one is inclined to perform the opening music of the Book brings with it a manner of playing that is firm and frequently heavy."

Editorial appendix

Critical Notes

The critical notes record minor corrections and discrepancies between the examples presented in the method and their original editions in the Books of Pieces. They indicate: bar number, affected part (RH = right hand, LH = left hand, voice), number of the modified note, and the correction.

★ How to read the critical notes

Example: 8, RH, 2nd voice, 4: D replacing E-sharp

= bar 8, right hand, alto, note 4: D replacing E-sharp.
