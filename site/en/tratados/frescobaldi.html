II

To the Reader

Girolamo Frescobaldi · Rome, 1637 · Preface to the Second Book of Toccatas

Girolamo Frescobaldi (Ferrara, 1583 — Rome, 1643) is, alongside Sweelinck, the most influential keyboard composer of the first half of the seventeenth century. His remarks To the Reader, though brief, are considered the most important sources for seventeenth-century keyboard practice.

📖 Historical context

The preface was published in several versions across the two books of Toccatas, from 1615 to 1637. This is the final version, from the Il secondo libro di toccate (Rome, 1637). Frescobaldi guides the performer through the new style of keyboard music — the stylus fantasticus — that he himself was creating. In 1637, Froberger travelled to Rome to become his pupil.

Listen before reading

Preface

"Being aware of how much the manner of playing with singing affects and with variety of passages is appreciated, it seemed worthwhile to show how favourable and well-disposed I am toward these my modest labours..." — Frescobaldi, opening of the preface

The preface consists of nine numbered points — precise instructions that unlock the schematic notation of the toccatas:

Point 1

Free time — as in madrigals

"First, this manner of playing must not be subject to the beat, as we see practised in modern madrigals which, though difficult, are made easier by the beat conducted now languidly, now quickly, and even suspended in the air according to their affects or the meaning of the words."

★ Fundamental principle

The tempo in Frescobaldi's toccatas is never literal. The pulse yields to the affect. The analogy with the madrigal — vocal music that follows the meaning of the text — authorises all subsequent rhythmic flexibility.

Point 2

Toccatas in autonomous passages

"In the toccatas, I have taken care not only that they be rich in varied passages and affects, but also that each of these passages may be played independently of the others, so that the performer is not obliged to finish them all to the end of the piece, but may stop wherever it seems fitting."

The toccatas are composed of relatively independent sections (passi). The performer may break off at cadential points — a remarkably modular concept for the modern listener accustomed to the closed narrative of a self-contained piece.

Point 3

Openings: arpeggios and suspensions

"The openings of the toccatas are to be played at ease and with arpeggiation; in this way, the suspensions or dissonances, both at the beginning and in the middle of the piece, are to be re-struck, so as not to leave the sound of the instrument empty. Such repetition shall be resumed at the pleasure of the player."

Since the sound of the harpsichord decays rapidly, Frescobaldi instructs that suspensions and dissonances be re-attacked — a "live" arpeggio to sustain the harmony. How many times to repeat is left to the performer's discretion.

Point 4

Pause after trills and passages

"On the last note, whether of trills or of passages by leap or by step, one must pause, even if that note is a quaver, or a semiquaver, or different from the one that follows, for this pause will prevent one passage from being confused with another."

Structural clarity demands small pauses between sections — even when the notation does not indicate them. Each passage has its own ending, and the performer must make it clear.

Point 5

Sustained cadences

"Cadences, even when written as fast notes, must be held very broadly; and as one approaches the end of passages or cadences, the tempo shall be sustained more slowly. The separation and conclusion of passages will occur when both hands arrive together at a consonance written in half notes (minims)."

A principle that would become universal: ritardando at cadences. Frescobaldi also provides a clear sign for detecting the ends of sections — when both hands arrive at a consonance in minims (half notes).

Point 6

Trill in one hand, passage in the other

"When a trill is found in the right hand, or likewise in the left, and at the same time a passage in the other hand, one must not divide note by note, but only endeavour to make the trill fast while the passage is performed expressively and less rapidly; otherwise there would be confusion."

★ Principle of hand independence

Do not attempt to synchronise trill and passage mathematically: let the trill run freely, and play the passage expressively. The listener's ear synthesises what appears to be separate into a coherent whole.

Point 7

Quavers and semiquavers together — the dot

"When a passage of quavers and semiquavers together is found in both hands, one must not play very fast; the hand playing the semiquavers should make them somewhat dotted — that is, not the first, but the second with a dot, and so all of them, alternating."

Here Frescobaldi proposes a Lombardic inequality — the second note longer, creating an effect almost of notes inégales in reverse. Compare with the elegance of Sancta Maria.

Point 8

Before double passages — stop

"Before performing double passages of semiquavers with both hands, one must pause on the preceding note, even if it is a black note. Then, resolutely, the passage shall be made, so as to enhance further the agility of the hand."

To impress the listener with agility in double passages, mark a brief suspension beforehand — the silence prepares the brilliance. A simple and effective rhetorical principle.

Point 9

Partitas, Passacaglias and Chaconnes

"In Partitas, when passages and affects are encountered, it will be good to adopt a slower tempo, which is also observed in the toccatas. The other sections that contain no passages may be played at a somewhat faster tempo, leaving to the good taste and fine judgement of the performer the conducting of the tempo, upon which depends the spirit and perfection of this manner and style of playing."
"The Passacaglias may be played separately, in whatever manner is most pleasing, adjusting the tempo from one section to another, as likewise the Chaconnes."

★ Historical note

By placing Passacaglia and Chaconne side by side in his Il secondo libro di toccate (1627), Frescobaldi became apparently the first composer to explore the potential of these forms as paired genres.

Synthetic principles

1 · Flexible tempo

The affects govern the pace. No metronome.

2 · Modular structure

Toccatas as mosaics of passages. One may stop at any cadential joint.

3 · Sustaining the sound

Re-attack dissonances, arpeggiate openings. The harpsichord has decay; counteract it.

4 · Slow cadences

Expressive ritardando at the end of each section, even when not written.

5 · Independence

Trill and passage in different hands? Do not synchronise: let them coexist.

6 · Good taste

"The fine judgement of the performer" — a principle that runs through Couperin and Rameau.
