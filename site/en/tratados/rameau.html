IV

On the Mechanics of the Fingers on the Harpsichord

Jean-Philippe Rameau · Paris, 1724 · De la mechanique des doigts sur le clavessin

Jean-Philippe Rameau (Dijon, 1683 — Paris, 1764), composer, theorist, organist, and harpsichordist. His "Mechanics" is considered "possibly the most precise description of French keyboard technique ever written" — analytical and rational, in keeping with the early years of the Enlightenment.

★ Rameau's defining statement

"It is not enough to feel the effects of a science or art; it is necessary to understand them in a way that makes them intelligible."
— Rameau, preface to the Treatise on Harmony (1722)

Founding principle

The analogy: walking and playing

"The perfection of touch on the Harpsichord consists principally in a well-guided movement of the fingers. [...] The ability to walk or to run comes from the flexibility of the knee joint; whereas the ability to play the Harpsichord depends on the flexibility of the fingers at their roots." — Rameau, opening

We all walk nearly equally well because the exercise is constant; almost no one moves their fingers properly at the keyboard because the exercise is rare. Worse still: "our particular habits cause the fingers to acquire movements so contrary to those required at the harpsichord that this freedom is constantly curtailed."

★ A psychology of practice

Rameau is the first to observe that expressive effort hinders performance: "If we are at all sensitive to the effects of this art, we shall make efforts to convey what we feel, and this can only lead to a tension that is harmful to performance."

"Frequent and well-understood practice is the infallible author of perfect performance on the Harpsichord."
Part 1

Position at the instrument

Finger numbering

The same numbering as Couperin: 1 = thumb, 5 = little finger.

The seat and the elbows

"One must, first of all, be seated at the Harpsichord in such a way that the elbows are higher than the level of the keyboard and that the hand may fall upon it solely through the natural movement of the wrist joint."

The elbows above the level of the keyboard — but never excessively so. A position such that fingers 1 and 5 can rest at the edge of the keys.

★ A brilliant detail

"The elbows fall naturally to the sides, in their natural position — a position that must be carefully observed and should never be altered except out of absolute necessity."

This "natural fixed point" of the elbow is what gives stability to the touch. Every person, "of whatever height," finds their position simply by adjusting the seat.

The curved fingers

"Fingers 1 and 5, being at the edge of the keys, cause the other fingers to curve" — upon letting the hand fall, the fingers round naturally.

The flexible wrist

"The wrist joint must always be flexible. [...] The hand, which by this means finds itself, so to speak, as if dead, serves only to support the fingers that are attached to it."

Part 2

The movement of the fingers — principles

1
Origin of movement
"The movement of the fingers originates at their root, that is, at the joint that connects them to the hand and never anywhere else." The hand moves at the wrist joint, the arm at the elbow.
2
Principle of economy
"The larger movement should only occur to the extent that a smaller one is not sufficient" — perhaps the most influential phrase in the treatise.
3
Complete independence
"It is essential that each finger have its own movement, independent of any other" — even when the hand shifts position.
4
Fall, do not strike
"It is important that the fingers fall upon the keys and not that they strike them. It is necessary that they flow, so to speak, from one to another in succession."

★ Compare with Couperin

Rameau, like Couperin, values legato and lightness. The difference lies in tone: Couperin is poetic; Rameau is mechanistic — yet both arrive at the same ideal of sound.

Part 3

The First Lesson — five fingers on five keys

"One must, for the moment, place the five fingers of the hand on five consecutive notes or keys."

Initial position

1 2 3 4 5

Five fingers on five consecutive white keys (C-D-E-F-G).

Right hand: 5 fingers on C-D-E-F-G

The First Lesson in facsimile (1724)

In the original table published with the Pièces de clavecin (1724), Rameau presents: ornaments with their names, expression, and examples; the Première Leçon (the First Lesson we are studying); and the Menuet en Rondeau recommended as the first piece to learn.

How to practise

  1. Depress with finger 1 or 5 the key on which it rests, without any other finger or the hand making the slightest movement.
  2. Pass to its neighbour, and so from one to the next.
  3. Critical coordination: "the one that has just depressed a key should release it at the very instant its neighbour depresses another; for the lifting of one finger and the striking of another must be executed at the same moment."
"Never weight the touch of your fingers through the effort of your hand; on the contrary, let your hand, by supporting your fingers, make their touch lighter. This is of great consequence."

★ Equality of movements

"Observe the great equality of movements between each finger and, above all, never rush those movements, for lightness and speed are achieved only through this equality of movements. Often, by hurrying too much, one flees from what one seeks."

Keyboard resistance

"Let the keyboard on which one practises not be considered too light. But as the fingers strengthen in their movements, one may present them with a less light keyboard, gradually working up to having them play the heavier keys."

Practising with both hands

First each hand individually; then together. "One begins with one hand before the other, with as many notes as one wishes, now more, now fewer; in short, this is done in every possible way."

The outcomes of this Lesson:

  • Accustom the hand to supporting the fingers.
  • Calibrate the distances between fingers to the distances between keys.
  • Give each finger its own movement.
  • Equalize the forces, weights, and movements among fingers.
  • Acquire equal and contrary movements between the hands.

After the First Lesson, one may learn the small Minuet en Rondeau included in the treatise's table, "having taken care to mark the fingerings and to omit the ornaments."

Part 4

Runs and batteries

Rameau introduces two technical concepts:

Run roulement

A rapid passage through the conjunct notes of the Lesson — a scale. For longer runs, the passing of the thumb is required.

Battery batterie

When the notes of the Lesson are played in leaps (disjunct notes) — a broken chord.

The passing of the thumb

"To continue a run longer than that of the Lesson, one need only accustom oneself to passing finger 1 under whichever other finger one wishes, and passing one of those other fingers over finger 1. This method is excellent, especially when sharps and flats are encountered."

★ Rameau and the thumb

Unlike Couperin (who prefers crossing finger 3 over finger 2 in the old style), Rameau prescribes only modern fingerings — in parallel with the developments of J. S. Bach in Germany. The passing of the thumb is central to his technique.

"Avoid, as much as possible, playing a sharp or flat with finger 1 or 5, especially in runs; and in such a way that finger 1 falls on the key preceding that sharp or flat, for this can greatly facilitate its execution."

Cross-handed batteries

Rameau presents technical innovations in two of his own pieces:

★ Les Tourbillons — run between the hands

"The hands pass one over the other, but it must be carefully observed that the sound of the first key struck by whichever hand crosses should be as legato with the preceding sound as if they were being played by fingers of the same hand." The letter D indicates the right hand (droite), G the left (gauche).

Les Tourbillons — recordings for comparison

★ Les Cyclopes — two innovations

"In one of these batteries, the hands make between them the consecutive motion of two drumsticks; in the other, the left hand passes over the right to strike alternately the bass and the upper part. I believe these latter batteries are my own invention — at least nothing of the kind has yet appeared — and I may say in their favour that the eye shares in the pleasure that the ear receives."

Les Cyclopes — 4 interpretations

"The execution of these different batteries and runs depends principally on the flexibility of the wrist, being guided by smooth and light movements, and maintaining the fixed point at the elbow joint when the battery exceeds the span of the hand."

Part 5

Trills and cadences

"When practising trills or cadences, one must raise, as high as possible, only the fingers being used at that moment. But as the movement becomes familiar, those fingers are raised less, and the large movement becomes, in the end, a lively and light movement."

★ A learning strategy

Begin large and exaggerated to fix the coordination; then gradually reduce the amplitude until obtaining a lively and light movement. This is the exact opposite of the student's initial instinct (which would be to begin small and tense).

"One must take care not to rush the cadence at the end in order to conclude it; it closes naturally once the habit has been acquired."

Conclusion

Final observations

Progressive seating

"When one feels that the hand is formed, the height of the seat is gradually lowered until the elbows are a little below the level of the keyboard. This is what leads, at that point, to keeping the hand as if glued to the keyboard, and ultimately imparts to the touch all the legato that can thus be introduced."

The elbows begin above the level of the keyboard (so that the hands "fall") and progressively lower as technique matures — ending below, a position that "glues" the hand to the keyboard and maximises legato.

On transpositions and adaptations

"There are some Pieces in this Book that may be transposed. For example, La Musette may be transposed to C sol ut, principally to be played with the viola, and Les Rigaudons to D la ré." — A practical note that speaks directly to the musical life of the amateur.

"When the hand cannot easily reach two keys together, one may omit the one that is not absolutely necessary to the melody, for one must not be bound by the impossible."

The harpsichord and the organ

"What I have said with respect to the Harpsichord is equally to be observed for the Organ."

Principles and perseverance

"Let it be well remembered that the more one perseveres in the first principles, the further one advances in the pursuit; for the person to whom these principles are disagreeable is almost always the victim of their own impatience." — Rameau, conclusion

Summary — five principles of Rameau

1
Natural movement
The hand "falls" onto the keyboard; the fingers curve naturally; the wrist is flexible.
2
Radical independence
Each finger moves at its root, independently of the hand and of the other fingers.
3
Economy of gesture
"The larger movement should only occur to the extent that a smaller one is not sufficient."
4
Equality of movements
Speed and lightness come from equality — not from forced acceleration.
5
Modernity of the thumb
Only modern fingerings: thumb-under passing as the central tool of scale playing.
