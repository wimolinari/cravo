Body and Hand Position

From Sancta Maria's cat's hand to Rameau's progressive elbows

The three authors who address posture — Sancta Maria, Couperin, Rameau — arrive at a surprising consensus: the hand should be supported by its own natural shape, curved as if holding a sphere. They differ in the strategies for getting there.

Cat's hand / cat's claw manos en garra (Sancta Maria, 1565)

"Hands in a claw, like cat's paws" — fingers arched as if holding a sphere, with no hump between the hand and fingers, and the knuckles sunken. The mental image is that of a cat in a pouncing posture — muscles firm but relaxed, ready to "spring" onto the keys.

🏹
Fingers like a drawn bow arqueados (Sancta Maria)

"The more they are extended, the more firmly they strike the keys" — Sancta Maria explicitly compares the fingers to a bow. Controlled tension (not rigidity) generates the "impetus" of the attack.

Couperin's mirror contre les grimaces (Couperin, 1717)

"As for grimaces of the face, one may correct oneself by placing a mirror on the music desk of the Spinet, or of the Harpsichord." — The image of the harpsichordist observing their own face while playing, in order to avoid grimaces, is one of the most memorable in the treatise.

The shape of the hands

Sancta Maria — "cat's paws"

"Hands in a claw, like cat's paws": fingers arched, with no hump between the hand and fingers. The fingers "higher than the hand", "like a drawn bow" — the more extended they are, the firmer the attack.

"Thumb dropped and folded inward; little finger drawn in until it almost touches the palm."

Couperin — do not stiffen

"Good execution depends far more on flexibility and great freedom of the fingers than on strength." Men who wish to attain perfection "should never subject their hands to any strenuous exercise."

"To correct grimaces: a mirror on the music desk. For a wrist held too high: a flexible rod."

Rameau — the "dead" hand

"The 1st and 5th being at the edge of the keys lead the other fingers to curve naturally [...] The wrist joint must always be flexible [...] The hand, which by this means finds itself, so to speak, as if dead, serves only to support the fingers."

The elbows

SM
Sancta Maria — close to the body, without tension
"The arms, at the inner part of the elbows, should be kept close to the body, but without any tension." They move away from the body only to ascend long passages (left hand) or descend (right hand).
FC
Couperin — forearm, wrist, and fingers level
"It is necessary that the lower part of the elbows, the wrists, and the fingers be level." The right chair height is what makes this horizontality possible.
JR
Rameau — progressive elbows
At the start: elbows above the level of the keyboard, "so that the hand may fall upon it solely by its natural movement." Later: the seat is lowered until the elbows are below the keyboard, "which ultimately imparts to the touch all its legato."

★ Rameau's key insight

The "high" elbow is a pedagogical stage, not the final position. Once the hand is properly formed, lowering the seat integrates the fingers into the keyboard and automatically creates legato. This progression is unique to Rameau.

The seat

Couperin — precise distances

  • Adult: approximately 22.5 cm from the waist to the keyboard.
  • Centre of the body aligned with the centre of the keyboard.
  • Feet well supported (a footstool for children).
  • Turn as little as possible to the right.

Rameau — a universal adjustment

The pairing of "1st and 5th fingers at the edge of the keys" + "relaxed elbows" provides the fixed point at which "every person, of whatever size, should be positioned at the harpsichord" — one need only adjust the seat accordingly.

The key attack

SM
Sancta Maria — fingertip, at the edge
Strike with the fingertips (not the nails), at the edges of the keys, lowering the wrists. "Do not attack from above" — keep the fingers close to the keys.
FC
Couperin — close, gentle
"The sweetness of touch depends further on keeping the fingers as close as possible to the keys. A hand that falls from a height gives a drier stroke."
JR
Rameau — fall, do not strike
"It is important that the fingers fall upon the keys and not that they strike them. It is necessary that they flow, so to speak, from one to another in succession."

★ Centuries of consensus

Three authors, three centuries, one idea: do not strike from above. Fingers close to the keys, a gentle attack. Even Sancta Maria, who prescribes striking "strongly and with impetus", insists that this be done with the fingers held low.

The question of age

Couperin — begin early

"The appropriate age for starting children is six to seven years: not that this should exclude older persons, but, naturally, to shape the hands in the practice of the Harpsichord, the earlier the better."

"Persons who begin late should loosen up before playing — stretching the fingers in every direction."

Rameau — everyone can

"Provided no perceptible discomfort disturbs the natural movement of the fingers, it depends above all on ourselves to make proper use of them. I venture to affirm that assiduous and well-directed practice, proper care, and a little time will inevitably compensate for the least favoured fingers."
