Articulation and touch

How to give soul to an instrument without dynamics

The harpsichord does not allow variation in intensity through key pressure — every note has the same volume. Expression therefore depends entirely on the time between notes: how long each sound is sustained, how much silence follows it, how it connects (or does not) to the next note. This is articulation.

The attack — converging principles

SM
Fingertip, impulse, edge
Sancta Maria: attack with the fingertip (not the nail); strong and firm; at the edge of the keys; with equality between both hands. Ch. XV.
FC
Gentleness, close to the keys
Couperin: fingers close to the keys ensure gentleness. Soft quills for beginners. Strength does not count — flexibility does.
JR
Fall, flow
Rameau: the fingers fall (do not strike) and flow from one to the other. The hand supports the fingers; never the reverse.

Legato — the predominant articulation

Sancta Maria — clarity and distinction

"The finger that attacks first shall be lifted before the one that follows [...] lifting each finger a little after having pressed the key." This is a legato with micro-articulation: notes connected, but with a small space to distinguish them.

Couperin — modern legato

Perfect legato becomes the ideal. Couperin laments the old manner (3 over 2 in thirds) "which produces no legato whatsoever"; he recommends finger substitution on the same note.

"Il faut conserver un legato parfait dans ce que l'on exécute." ("One must maintain a perfect legato in everything one performs.")

Rameau — legato as result

"He who has just depressed a key shall release it at the very instant his neighbour depresses another." The simultaneity of lifting and attacking produces legato. The gradual lowering of the seat "ultimately provides the touch with all its legato."

★ The evolution of the ideal

1565: legato with small separations between notes, characteristic of the polyphonic style.
1717: perfect legato as ideal; finger substitution as technique.
1724: legato as the mechanical consequence of correct posture.

Cantabile affects — the soul of Frescobaldi

"Havendo consideratione di quanto sia gradita la maniera di sonar con affetti cantabili et con diversità di passaggi..." — Frescobaldi, opening of the preface (1637)

Frescobaldi does not speak of articulation in the technical sense, but provides principles of expression that depend entirely upon it:

  • Repeating ties / dissonances to sustain the sound — articulating in order to re-attack.
  • Stopping on the last note of trills and passages — small pauses that separate sections.
  • Fast trill + affective passage in opposite hands — rhythmic independence as articulation.
  • Pause before double passages — rhetorical silence that heightens the brilliance that follows.

Aspiration and Suspension — articulation as expression

Couperin elevated articulation to the level of ornament. Aspiration and Suspension are not melodic embellishments — they are calculated silences:

Aspiration

Before a note, the performer releases the preceding note slightly in advance. The result: the following note seems to "breathe."

"More intense in light and rapid passages; less so in gentle and slow ones."

Suspension

Before a note, the performer inserts a silence. The result: the following note gains gravity and emotional presence.

"Almost exclusively in gentle and slow pieces. Guided by the taste of the performer."

"Les sons du Clavecin étant chacun fixés en eux-mêmes, et par conséquent ne pouvant être enflez ni diminuez, il avoit paru jusqu'ici presque insoutenable qu'on pût donner de l'âme à cet instrument. [Mais] l'expression expressive doit son résultat à la cessation et à la suspension des sons, faites au moment convenable." ("The sounds of the harpsichord being each fixed in themselves, and consequently incapable of being swelled or diminished, it had seemed almost impossible until now that one could give soul to this instrument. [But] the expressive effect owes its result to the cessation and suspension of sounds, made at the right moment.") — Couperin

Synthesis

The Baroque harpsichord — according to its four treatises — does not seek the uniform legato of the Romantic piano. It seeks a variable, articulated, rhetorical legato: each note carries its own weight, each phrase breathes, each cadence is sustained. Articulation is the dynamics of the harpsichord.
