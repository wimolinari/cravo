Theme

Ornaments

Redouble (redobro), quebro (Spanish ornament), mordents, appoggiaturas, trills — in 4 voices

Ornaments are the heart of expressiveness on the harpsichord. Each author treats them with their own vocabulary, yet it is possible to map correspondences and differences. This page presents the principal ornaments visually, in parallel across the four treatises.

The Trill — alternation of the note with the one above

Sancta Maria (1565)

Quebro Reiterado = simple trill (e–f–e–f–e). Performed on Minims.

e–f–e–f–e–f–e

"Modern" start: beginning from the note above, first note before the beat. Already in 1565!

Frescobaldi (1637)

Does not define visually, but gives rules of execution:

  • "The trill should be fast, and the passage [in the other hand] should be played with feeling and somewhat less quickly."
  • Do not divide note by note; allow both rhythms to coexist.

Couperin (1717)

Three phases: (1) appui on the upper note; (2) repercussions; (3) point d'arrêt.

"They must begin more slowly than they end, but this gradation must be imperceptible." Always begins on the upper note.

Rameau (1724)

For practice: lift the fingers very high at the start; then reduce to a "lively and light motion".

"Do not rush the cadence at the end in order to conclude it; it closes naturally."

★ Principle common to all 4

All four begin the trill on the upper note (not the principal note). This rule persists until around 1800.

The trill in performance

The Mordent — alternation with the note below

Sancta Maria (1565)

Redouble (redobro) = reiterated mordent (e–d–e–f–e–f–e). Performed on Semibreves. Involves both the lower and upper neighbor.

Simple quebro (Spanish ornament) = short mordent (3 notes: e–f–e). Performed on Crotchets.

Couperin (1717)

Pincé / pincé-double (mordent / double mordent).

"Every mordent must end on the note over which it is placed." The repercussions and the point d'arrêt are all contained within the value of the note.

"The double mordent, in the playing of the organ and the harpsichord, serves the same function as the martèlement on bowed instruments."

★ How to connect consecutive mordents

Couperin describes a highly refined technique: linking mordents on conjunct degrees by changing finger on the same note. This is one of the highlights of the treatise.

The Appoggiatura — ornamental note before the principal note

Couperin (1717) — Port-de-voix

Composed of two notes of value plus one "small grace note".

Couperin presents an old manner and a modern manner; he recommends the modern one because it allows more legato (with a change of finger).

"I have observed that, without seeing the hands of the person playing, I can tell whether the repetition of the two notes in question was made by the same finger or by two different fingers."

Aspiration and Suspension — exclusive to Couperin

Aspiration

Intentional separation of a note — a micro-pause that gives air to the phrase. More pronounced in light, fast passages; more subtle in gentle and slow passages.

Suspension

Silence before a note, in gentle and slow pieces. "The silence that precedes the note should be guided by the taste of the performer."

"Ces deux agréments, par leur contraste, laissent l'oreille incertaine, de sorte que dans les occasions où les instruments à archet enflent leurs sons, la suspension des sons du Clavecin semble — par un effet contraire — apporter à l'oreille l'impression désirée." ("These two ornaments, by their contrast, leave the ear uncertain, so that on the occasions when bowed instruments swell their sounds, the suspension of the harpsichord's sounds seems — by a contrary effect — to bring to the ear the desired impression.") — Couperin

The ornament as rhetoric

For all four authors, the ornament is not decoration: it is an essential part of musical rhetoric. Sancta Maria states that without ornaments "the music seems to be a different thing altogether"; Couperin affirms that ornaments "give soul" to the harpsichord. Good execution depends on:

  • Rhythmic precision — each ornament has its place and duration.
  • Beginning on the correct note — almost always the upper note in trills.
  • Avoid excess — Sancta Maria: "the Redoubles (redobros) must by no means be made too long, for they disfigure the music."
  • Adapting to the character of the piece and the tempo.
