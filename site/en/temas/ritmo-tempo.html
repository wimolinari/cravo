Theme

Rhythm, Tempo, and Expression

From Sancta Maria's elegance to Rameau's equality

The central question: what is written is not what is played. The four authors negotiate in different ways the tension between rigid notation and living performance. Sancta Maria proposes three types of inégalité; Frescobaldi liberates time; Couperin distinguishes meter from cadence; Rameau demands rigorous equality from the student — and velocity as its consequence.

Rhythmic inequalities — notes inégales

Sancta Maria (1565) — buen ayre

The seventh of the eight conditions. Three ways of treating quavers:

  1. Dot the 1st, run the 2nd (standard style).
  2. Run the 1st, dot the 2nd (Lombardy style — "much more graceful").
  3. Run 3, dot the 4th ("the most graceful of all").

Frescobaldi (1637) — reversed dotting

When quavers and semiquavers coincide in both hands: "not the first, but the second with a dot, and so all of them, one without and one with".

This is a Lombard pattern, extremely rare elsewhere in seventeenth-century Europe.

Couperin (1717) — theoretical basis of French notes inégales

"We dot several consecutive quavers moving by step, yet we notate them as equal."

The listener of good taste distinguishes between what is written and what is performed.

★ Important principle

Sancta Maria warns: "the lingering on the quavers must not be too much, but only hinted at and made slightly perceptible, because an excessively long pause brings disgrace and ugliness to the music". Notes inégales are subtle, not dramatic.

Free tempo in toccatas

"This manner of playing must not be subject to the beat, as we see practiced in modern madrigals which, although difficult, are made easier by the beat conducted now languishing, now fast and even suspended in the air according to their affections or the sense of the words." — Frescobaldi, point 1

Frescobaldi establishes the toccata–madrigal analogy: instrumental music can (and should) flex itself as vocal music does, guided by the affection.

In Couperin: meter vs. cadence

★ Foundational distinction

Meter = "the quantity and equality of beats".

Cadence = "precisely, the spirit and soul that must be combined with [meter]".

"We confuse meter with what is called cadence or movement."

This is the first clear theorization of rubato as an expressive principle: there is the pulse (meter) and there is the soul of the pulse (cadence). To play well is to negotiate between the two.

Rameau — absolute equality in practice

Rameau takes the opposite path:

"Observe the great equality of movements between each finger and, above all, never hurry these movements, for lightness and velocity are obtained only through this equality of movements. By hurrying too much, one often flees from the very thing one seeks." — Rameau

For Rameau, the final expression may include flexibility — but the technical foundation is rigorous equality. Velocity is a consequence; rushing, its enemy.

Sustained cadences — a universal principle

FB
Frescobaldi (Point 5)
"Cadences, even if written quickly, must be very sustained; and when approaching the end of passages or cadences, one plays sustaining the tempo more slowly."
FC
Couperin
"Care must be taken not to alter the tempo in pieces when it has been set, and not to linger on notes whose duration has ended." More sober than Frescobaldi.
JR
Rameau
"Care must be taken not to hurry the cadence at the end in order to conclude it; it closes naturally once the habit has been acquired."

The character of a piece — words as guide

The French authors cultivate verbal indications for character:

Couperin — Tendrement, Vivement

"Not having devised signs or characters to communicate our particular ideas, we have tried to remedy this by means of a few words placed at the beginning of pieces such as Tendrement, Vivement etc., which approximates the way we would wish to make ourselves understood."

Couperin — the character of the harpsichord

"One must not depart from the character that befits this instrument: passages and batteries that lie well in the hands; lute-style and syncopated parts, which are to be preferred to those that are full of ties or excessively low notes."

The Preludes — prose and verse

"One may venture to say that, among many subjects, Music, compared to Poetry, has its prose and its verse." — Couperin on the Preludes

Preludes are prose (free); most pieces are verse (measured). When Couperin writes Mesuré in a prelude, he is, exceptionally, "versifying".

Synthesis — principles for today's student

  • The metronome is not the judge. Affection guides tempo (Frescobaldi).
  • But regularity is the foundation. Study with equality (Rameau); perform with cadence (Couperin).
  • Cadences breathe. Sustain the tempo at the end of phrases (Frescobaldi).
  • Notes inégales are subtle. They are not dramatic dotting (Sancta Maria).
  • Preludes are prose. Pieces are verse. Know which is which (Couperin).
  • Trust the character of the piece more than the notation. The words (Tendrement, Vivement) and the genre (Allemande, Gigue, Passacaglia) already say a great deal.
