Fingering

The four authors in dialogue · from early to modern fingering

The history of keyboard fingering between 1565 and 1724 is the history of the transition from old to modern — from the "3 over 2" pair to the thumb passing under the fingers. The four treatises gathered here cover exactly that arc.

★ The great revolution

Modern fingering (with thumb-under passing) already existed in 1565: Sancta Maria describes it clearly. But it was only with Rameau (1724) and Bach in Germany that it became the rule. Couperin (1717) stands exactly at the midpoint: he uses the thumb in his pieces, but still teaches the old "3 over 2" fingering as valid.

Finger Numbering

The treatises use different conventions:

Sancta Maria (1565)

Thumb = 1, little finger = 5. Same as the modern convention. Literal quotation: "from the thumb finger… counting successively from one to five."

Couperin (1717)

"I establish with regard to this method — unlike my usual practice — that one shall begin by counting the thumb of each hand as the first finger." Couperin had previously used a different numbering; here he adopts the modern one.

Rameau (1724)

"The numbers 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 shall designate the fingers: 1 shall designate the thumb, 5 the little finger." The convention that endures to this day.

Sancta Maria (1565) — right hand descending in Quavers (Eighth Notes)

Variation 2, with thumb-under passing:

321321...

"As a general rule […] when descending with the right hand playing Crotchets or Quavers, one shall strike with the thumb finger and immediately after it the middle finger shall follow." (Ch. XVIII)

Sancta Maria (1565) — left hand in Quavers (Eighth Notes)

Four fingers with thumb-under passing:

Ascending: 43214321
Descending: 12341234

"This rule must be held in great esteem, for it is very good and necessary." (Ch. XVIII)

Couperin (1717) — slurred thirds, modern manner

Couperin laments that "the old manner produces no legato whatsoever"; he recommends finger substitution on the same note in order to slur consecutive thirds — but still based on "3 over 2" / "4 over 3" pairs in the French style.

"I am persuaded that few people in Paris remain unconvinced about old principles."

Rameau (1724) — thumb-under passing as the rule

"To continue a passage longer than that of the Lesson, one need only accustom oneself to passing the 1 under whichever other finger one wishes, and to passing one of those other fingers over the 1."

"Avoid, as much as possible, playing a sharp or flat with the 1 or 5."

Summary: the evolution over 159 years

1565
Sancta Maria — all the options
Complete system: 3-4 for short scale passages (the "general" rule), with possibilities for 1-2-3-4 + thumb-under passing for longer passages. Pragmatic and didactic.
1637
Frescobaldi — makes no mention
Does not detail fingerings (focuses on style and tempo). Seventeenth-century Italian practice favours the "3 over 2" crossing.
1717
Couperin — transition
Retains the "3 over 2" crossing as the standard fingering; introduces finger substitution for legato; uses the thumb when necessary, but not as a governing principle.
1724
Rameau — modern only
Only modern fingerings. The thumb is the key to scale playing; old crossings are abandoned. In parallel with J. S. Bach in Germany.
